Tiny but Mighty: Super-Short, Super-Clever Algorithms

Created on November 14, 2026

2026   ·   rust   algorithms   ·   notes

These notes collect a few pearls: problems with a short statement and a solution that looks like magic the first time you see it. They have something in common. In several of them we look at an array of numbers as a set of arrows, from every position to the value stored there. Cycles in this picture save 100 imprisoned scientists, reveal a duplicated number, and are found by two pointers running at different speeds. The last problems are about counting: we find the element that occurs in more than half of an array, and then the elements that occur often, in constant space or almost.

The 100 Prisoners Problem

The 100 Prisoners Problem

The director of a prison offers a last chance of freedom to \(100\) scientists held there. He assigns to each scientist a distinct number in \(\{1, \ldots, 100\}.\)

In a room there is a cabinet with \(100\) drawers. The director puts the numbers \(1, \ldots, 100\) in the drawers in random order, one number per drawer, every arrangement being equally likely.

One at a time, each scientist enters the room alone and can open up to \(50\) drawers. He wins if he finds his own number. Then the drawers are closed again and the room is left as it was.

The scientists are freed only if all of them win. They cannot communicate once the game starts, but they may agree on a strategy before.

A trivial strategy

Each scientist opens \(50\) drawers chosen uniformly at random. A scientist wins with probability \(50/100 = 1/2.\) The scientists choose independently, so

\[P(\text{all win}) = \frac{1}{2^{100}} \approx 7.9 \cdot 10^{-31},\]

that is, \(30\) zeros after the decimal point before the first nonzero digit. Hopeless. Yet there is a strategy with which they all win with probability about \(0.3118.\) Before reading on, think about how this could be possible: every single scientist still opens only half of the drawers.

The cycle strategy

Two observations lead to the strategy.

First, a scientist does not need to choose his \(50\) drawers beforehand. He can use an adaptive strategy: the next drawer to open may depend on the numbers found so far.

Second, not only the scientists are numbered: the drawers can be numbered too, for example row-wise from left to right. So a number found in a drawer can be read as the name of another drawer.

The strategy is as follows.

  • Scientist \(i\) opens drawer \(i,\) and finds a number \(d_i.\)
  • If \(d_i = i,\) he wins.
  • Otherwise, he opens drawer \(d_i,\) and continues in the same way, until he finds \(i\) or he has opened \(50\) drawers.

Let us follow it on a smaller instance, which we use as the running example of this section: \(8\) scientists, \(8\) drawers, and each scientist may open up to \(4\) drawers, that is, half of them. The drawers \(1, 2, \ldots, 8\) contain the numbers \([7, 4, 6, 8, 2, 3, 5, 1],\) as in the figure below. An arrow goes from drawer \(d\) to the drawer whose number is inside \(d.\)

Scientist \(1\) opens drawer \(1\) and finds \(7,\) then drawer \(7\) and finds \(5,\) and so on: he visits \(1 \to 7 \to 5 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 1.\) He finds his number in drawer \(8,\) but only at the \(6\)th drawer he opens, too late: he may open only \(4.\)

Observation. If we do not stop him after \(50\) drawers (here, \(4\)), the scientist is guaranteed to find his number.

Why? Every drawer contains a different number, so every drawer is pointed to by exactly one arrow. Follow the arrows from drawer \(i.\) There are finitely many drawers, so sooner or later the scientist opens some drawer for the second time; let \(j\) be the first drawer that he opens twice. Suppose \(j \neq i.\) Then \(j\) is not the starting drawer, so both times it was reached through an arrow: from the drawer opened just before \(j\) the first time, and from the drawer opened just before \(j\) the second time. These two drawers are different, because no drawer had been opened twice before the second visit of \(j.\) So two different drawers contain the same number \(j,\) which is not possible. The figure below shows the shape that cannot exist.

So the first drawer opened twice is drawer \(i\) itself, and the drawer opened just before it contains \(i.\) In other words, the drawers split into cycles: the arrows of a permutation always form disjoint cycles. Scientist \(i\) walks along the cycle of drawer \(i,\) and he finds his number at the last drawer of that cycle. He wins if and only if the length of his cycle is at most \(50.\)

In our example the cycles are \((1, 7, 5, 2, 4, 8),\) of length \(6,\) and \((3, 6),\) of length \(2.\)

Scientists \(3, 6\) win, and scientists \(1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8\) lose. This is the key of the strategy: the scientists do not fail independently. If a cycle is longer than \(50,\) every scientist on that cycle fails; if no cycle is longer than \(50,\) every scientist wins. Thus

\[P(\text{all win}) = P(\text{a random permutation of } 100 \text{ elements has no cycle longer than } 50).\]
Counting the permutations with a long cycle

Let us count the permutations of \(100\) elements with a cycle of length \(\ell > 50.\) Two cycles longer than \(50\) would need more than \(100\) elements, so such a permutation has only one long cycle. We can build it in three steps.

  • Choose the set \(S\) of the \(\ell\) elements of the cycle: \(\binom{100}{\ell}\) ways.
  • Arrange the elements of \(S\) in a cycle: \((\ell-1)!\) ways. Indeed, there are \(\ell!\) ways to list the elements of \(S\) in a sequence and to close the sequence into a cycle, but the \(\ell\) rotations of a sequence give the same cycle. The figure shows the \(4\) rotations of the cycle on \(S = \{1, 5, 10, 33\}.\)
  • Arrange the other \(100 - \ell\) elements as we like: \((100-\ell)!\) ways.

Since the long cycle is unique, every permutation with a cycle of length \(\ell\) is built exactly once. (For \(\ell \leq 50\) this would not be true: a permutation with two cycles of length \(\ell\) would be built twice.)

The number of permutations with a cycle of length \(\ell > 50\) is

\[\binom{100}{\ell} \cdot (\ell-1)! \cdot (100-\ell)! = \frac{100!}{\ell!\,(100-\ell)!} \cdot (\ell-1)! \cdot (100-\ell)! = \frac{100!}{\ell}.\]

Since a permutation has at most one cycle longer than \(50,\) the events “there is a cycle of length \(\ell\)”, for \(\ell = 51, \ldots, 100,\) are disjoint, and we can sum. The number of permutations with a cycle longer than \(50\) is

\[\sum_{\ell=51}^{100} \frac{100!}{\ell} = 100! \left(\frac{1}{51} + \frac{1}{52} + \cdots + \frac{1}{100}\right) = 100! \, (H_{100} - H_{50}),\]

where \(H_n = 1 + 1/2 + \cdots + 1/n\) is the \(n\)th harmonic number. We divide by the \(100!\) permutations and get

\[P(\text{all win}) = 1 - (H_{100} - H_{50}) \approx 1 - 0.6882 = 0.3118.\]

The same count works for our running example, with \(8\) scientists and \(4\) drawers each. The permutations with a cycle longer than \(4\) are \(8064 + 6720 + 5760 + 5040 = 25584\) out of \(8! = 40320,\) so the scientists win with probability \(14736/40320 \approx 0.3655.\)

Two remarks help to understand why the strategy works so well.

  • Each single scientist still wins with probability exactly \(1/2\) (for \(n\) even). The cycle containing a given element \(x\) has length \(\ell\) with probability \(1/n,\) for every \(\ell = 1, \ldots, n.\) Let us count as before, with \(x\) fixed in the cycle: we choose the other \(\ell - 1\) elements of the cycle, \(\binom{n-1}{\ell-1}\) ways, and the order in which they follow \(x\) along the cycle, \((\ell-1)!\) ways (no rotations to divide by, because we read the cycle starting from \(x\)), and we arrange the rest, \((n-\ell)!\) ways. In total \(\binom{n-1}{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!\,(n-\ell)! = (n-1)!\) permutations out of \(n!,\) a fraction \(1/n,\) whatever \(\ell\) is. So the length is at most \(n/2\) with probability \(1/2.\) The strategy does not make a scientist luckier; it makes the scientists win or lose together.
  • For \(2k\) scientists, each opening \(k\) drawers, the probability is \(1 - (H_{2k} - H_k).\) From \(k\) to \(k+1,\) \(H_{2k} - H_k\) grows by \(\frac{1}{2k+1} - \frac{1}{2k+2} > 0,\) and it tends to \(\ln 2.\) So the probability decreases towards \(1 - \ln 2 \approx 0.3069\) and never drops below it, however many scientists there are.

Finding a Duplicate in an Array

Finding a Duplicate

We are given an array \(A[0, n]\) of \(n+1\) numbers in \(\{0, \ldots, n-1\}.\) By the pigeonhole principle, at least one element is duplicated. Find one duplicate \(e.\)

Here positions start from \(0,\) as in the code. Our running example is

\[A = [1, 3, 5, 6, 5, 2, 0, 4, 7],\]

with \(n = 8\) and duplicate \(e = 5.\)

Two easy solutions

Solution 1. Use a hash set \(H.\) Scan \(A\) from left to right and stop when the current element is already in \(H;\) otherwise insert it.

Solution 2. The values are in \(\{0, \ldots, n-1\},\) so a hash set is not needed: a direct access table \(B[0, n-1]\) of bits does the job. \(B[k] = 1\) means that we have already seen value \(k.\)

/// `a` has n+1 entries in {0, ..., n-1}. Direct access table: Θ(n) time, Θ(n) extra space.
/// Requires n >= 1 and every value in 0..n.
pub fn duplicate_table(a: &[usize]) -> usize {
    let n = a.len() - 1;
    debug_assert!(n >= 1 && a.iter().all(|&x| x < n));
    let mut seen = vec![false; n];
    for &x in a {
        if seen[x] {
            return x;
        }
        seen[x] = true;
    }
    unreachable!("by the pigeonhole principle there is a duplicate")
}

Both take \(O(n)\) time, \(\Theta(n)\) in the worst case (expected time for the hash set), but \(\Theta(n)\) extra space.

Can we use only constant extra space? It seems impossible, because we believe we need to keep track of the elements we have seen so far.

\(\Theta(\log n)\) passes over \(A\)

We do not need to remember the elements. We can reconstruct \(e\) bit by bit, with \(\Theta(\log n)\) passes over \(A,\) each one reading \(A\) from left to right.

Write the numbers in binary with the same number of bits, \(3\) in our example (with leading zeros), and look at the first, most significant, bit. The numbers whose first bit is \(0\) are \(0, 1, 2, 3:\) only \(4\) distinct values. If \(A\) contained more than \(4\) elements starting with bit \(0,\) by the pigeonhole principle one of them would be duplicated. The same holds for the \(4\) values starting with bit \(1.\) The \(9\) elements of \(A\) cannot fit in both halves without exceeding one of them.

So in the first pass we count the elements whose first bit is \(0\) and those whose first bit is \(1:\) they are \(4\) and \(5.\) The second half holds \(5\) elements but only \(4\) values: a duplicate starts with \(1.\) In the second pass we look only at the numbers starting with \(1:\) \(3\) of them continue with \(0\) (values \(4, 5\)) and \(2\) with \(1\) (values \(6, 7\)). There are \(3\) elements for \(2\) values, so the second bit of a duplicate is \(0.\) In the third pass, among the numbers starting with \(10,\) \(1\) continues with \(0\) (value \(4\)) and \(2\) with \(1\) (value \(5\)): the duplicate is \(101_2 = 5.\) The figure shows the three passes; the elements outside the current range are grey, and the bit chosen at each pass is red.

When \(n\) is not a power of \(2,\) the halves defined by the bits may contain values larger than \(n-1.\) It is simpler to describe the same algorithm as a binary search over the range of values. We keep a range \([lo, hi]\) of values with the invariant

the number of elements of \(A\) in \([lo, hi]\) is larger than \(hi - lo + 1.\)

At the beginning \([lo, hi] = [0, n-1],\) which holds all the \(n+1\) elements and \(n\) values. At every pass we split the range at \(mid = \lfloor (lo+hi)/2 \rfloor\) and count the elements in \([lo, mid].\) If they are more than \(mid - lo + 1,\) we continue on \([lo, mid].\) Otherwise the elements in \([mid+1, hi]\) are more than

\[\begin{aligned} &(hi - lo + 1) - (mid - lo + 1) \\ &\quad = hi - mid, \end{aligned}\]

the number of values in \([mid+1, hi],\) and we continue there. The invariant is preserved, and when the range contains a single value, that value occurs at least twice. When \(n\) is a power of \(2,\) this is exactly the bit-by-bit algorithm.

Notice the rule: we compare the number of elements in a half with the number of values that half contains, not with the number of elements in the other half. When the two halves contain the same number of values, as when \(n\) is a power of \(2,\) the two rules agree: the half with more elements is the one that exceeds. When the halves are unbalanced, they do not. For instance, with \(n = 5\) and \(A = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 4],\) the values are in \([0, 4]:\) the first half \([0, 2]\) holds \(3\) elements for \(3\) values, and the second half \([3, 4]\) holds \(3\) elements for only \(2\) values, so we continue on \([3, 4];\) there, \([3, 3]\) holds \(1\) element and \([4, 4]\) holds \(2\) elements for a single value. In this last pass the half with more elements is also the one that exceeds, but in the first pass the two halves have the same number of elements and only the values tell them apart.

/// Θ(log n) read-only, left-to-right passes over `a`; O(1) extra space.
/// Invariant: more than hi - lo + 1 entries of `a` lie in [lo, hi],
/// so [lo, hi] contains a duplicate.
/// Requires n >= 1 and every value in 0..n.
pub fn duplicate_passes(a: &[usize]) -> usize {
    let n = a.len() - 1;
    debug_assert!(n >= 1 && a.iter().all(|&x| x < n));
    let (mut lo, mut hi) = (0, n - 1);
    while lo < hi {
        let mid = lo + (hi - lo) / 2;
        let count = a.iter().filter(|&&x| lo <= x && x <= mid).count();
        if count > mid - lo + 1 {
            hi = mid; // too many entries for the values in [lo, mid]
        } else {
            lo = mid + 1; // then [mid + 1, hi] has too many
        }
    }
    lo
}

The range halves at every pass, so we do \(\lceil \log_2 n \rceil\) passes of \(\Theta(n)\) time each: \(\Theta(n \log n)\) time and \(O(1)\) extra space. The algorithm only reads \(A\) sequentially, from left to right, and it never writes. This makes it the solution of choice in constant space when we have no random access to \(A,\) for example when \(A\) is a stream that we can read again and again.

Does random access help?

Let us look at \(A\) as a set of arrows: from every position \(i\) to position \(A[i].\) Every position has exactly one outgoing arrow. A value \(v\) is duplicated exactly when position \(v\) has two (or more) incoming arrows. The figure shows the arrows of our example.

Position \(n\) is special: no element of \(A\) has value \(n,\) so no arrow enters it. Let us start from position \(n\) and follow the arrows. As in the prisoners problem, sooner or later we reach a position for the second time. This time it cannot be the starting position, because nothing points to \(n.\) So the first position reached twice has two different incoming arrows: it is a duplicate. This is precisely the shape that could not exist for the scientists.

If we may destroy \(A,\) we can mark the positions we have visited without extra space. When we leave position \(j,\) we write \(j\) into \(A[j].\) When we arrive at a position \(v\) with \(A[v] = v,\) we stop and return \(v.\) Why is \(v\) a duplicate?

  • If we had already visited \(v,\) then we entered \(v\) twice from two different positions, which both contained \(v.\)
  • Otherwise \(A[v] = v\) held from the beginning. Since we arrived at \(v\) from another position that contained \(v,\) again \(v\) occurs twice.

On our example we start at position \(8\) and visit \(8 \to 7 \to 4 \to 5 \to 2 \to 5.\) When we come back to position \(5\) we find \(A[5] = 5,\) written when we left it, and we return \(e = 5.\) The numbers on the arcs are the order of the steps; the second row is \(A\) at the end.

/// Θ(n) time, O(1) extra space, but `a` is destroyed.
/// We follow the arrows i -> a[i] from position n, which no arrow enters.
/// Leaving position j we write j into a[j]. If we arrive at a position v with
/// a[v] == v, two different positions contain v, so v is a duplicate.
/// Requires n >= 1 and every value in 0..n.
pub fn duplicate_destroy(a: &mut [usize]) -> usize {
    debug_assert!(a.len() >= 2 && a.iter().all(|&x| x < a.len() - 1));
    let mut j = a.len() - 1; // j = n
    loop {
        let v = a[j];
        a[j] = j; // mark position j as visited
        if a[v] == v {
            return v;
        }
        j = v;
    }
}

We leave every position at most once, so this takes \(O(n)\) time (\(\Theta(n)\) in the worst case) and \(O(1)\) extra space, but it destroys \(A.\)

Can we have both, \(\Theta(n)\) time and constant space, without writing into \(A\)? Look again at the walk from position \(n:\) a path, followed by a cycle. The duplicate is the position where the path enters the cycle. We need to find the beginning of a cycle in a read-only structure.

Floyd’s Cycle Finding

Beginning of a Cycle

We are given a linked list that, after some nodes, enters a cycle. Find the beginning of the cycle in \(O(1)\) extra space. The list is read-only.

Let \(a\) be the number of nodes before the cycle (the length of the tail) and \(\ell\) the length of the cycle. In the list of the figure below, the nodes are numbered \(0, 1, \ldots, 10\) in the order of the list, \(a = 5\) and \(\ell = 6,\) and we must find node \(5.\) The red marks are explained below.

We use two pointers that start from the first node:

  • \(S,\) the slow pointer, moves just \(1\) step at a time;
  • \(F,\) the fast pointer, moves \(2\) steps at a time.

Phase 1. We move both pointers, at least once, until they are on the same node again. They meet in the cycle, which also certifies that a cycle exists. Indeed, \(F\) enters the cycle first and stays there. When \(S\) enters it too (if \(a \geq 1;\) the case \(a = 0\) is discussed below), \(F\) is at most \(\ell - 1\) nodes behind \(S\) along the cycle, and at every step it gets one node closer, so it reaches \(S\) within \(\ell - 1\) further steps, before \(S\) completes a loop.

Phase 2. We move \(F\) back to the first node and set its speed to \(1.\) We move both pointers until they meet again. Magic: they meet at the beginning of the cycle!

The table shows the positions of the two pointers after each step on the list of the figure; the meeting points are in bold.

Why it is correct

Suppose that, when they meet in Phase 1, \(S\) has moved by \(m\) steps. Then \(F\) has moved by \(2m\) steps. \(S\) has done \(a\) steps on the tail and \(b\) steps inside the cycle, so

\[m = a + b.\]

\(F\) is at the same node of the cycle. Inside the cycle it has done \(2m - a\) steps and \(S\) only \(b,\) and two walks along a cycle of length \(\ell\) from the same start end on the same node only if their lengths differ by a multiple of \(\ell.\) So \(F\) has done some full loops more, say \(k,\) and \(k \geq 1\) because \(2m - a > b\) (as \(m \geq 1\)):

\[2m = a + b + k \cdot \ell.\]

Substituting the first equation into the second one, \(2a + 2b = a + b + k\ell,\) that is,

\[a = k\ell - b.\]

Now read this equation in Phase 2. \(F\) starts from the first node and, after \(a\) steps, it is at the beginning of the cycle. \(S\) starts from the meeting point, which is \(b\) steps after the beginning of the cycle, and after \(a = k\ell - b\) steps it is \(b + k\ell - b = k\ell\) steps after the beginning of the cycle, that is, at the beginning of the cycle again, after \(k\) full loops. So after exactly \(a\) steps they are on the same node, the beginning of the cycle. They cannot meet before, because before \(a\) steps \(F\) is still on the tail and \(S\) is in the cycle.

In our list \(m = 6,\) \(a = 5,\) \(b = 1,\) \(\ell = 6\) and \(k = 1:\) indeed \(2 \cdot 6 = 5 + 1 + 1 \cdot 6\) and \(5 = 1 \cdot 6 - 1.\)

As for the cost, we saw that \(S\) meets \(F\) before completing a loop of the cycle, so \(b < \ell\) and Phase 1 takes \(m = a + b < a + \ell\) steps; Phase 2 takes \(a\) steps. (If \(a = 0\) the pointers start inside the cycle together, and they meet again after exactly \(\ell\) steps, at the start: \(b = \ell,\) and Phase 2 takes no step.) The time is \(\Theta(a + \ell),\) linear in the number of nodes, and the extra space is two pointers.

Back to the duplicate

The walk from position \(n\) of the previous section is a list: the next node of \(i\) is \(A[i],\) and we can compute it without writing anything. So we run Floyd’s algorithm from position \(n.\) The beginning of the cycle has two incoming arrows: one from the tail (the tail is not empty, because \(n\) is not on the cycle, as nothing points to \(n\)) and one from the cycle. So it is a duplicate.

On our example, \(a = 3\) (positions \(8, 7, 4\)), \(\ell = 2\) (positions \(5, 2\)), the pointers meet at position \(2\) after \(m = 4\) steps, and Phase 2 ends at position \(5\) after \(3\) steps. Here \(b = 1\) and \(k = 2:\) \(F\) went around the short cycle twice.

/// Floyd's cycle finding on the list x0, f(x0), f(f(x0)), ...
/// Returns the first node of the cycle. O(1) extra space; f is only read.
pub fn floyd<F: Fn(usize) -> usize>(f: F, x0: usize) -> usize {
    // Phase 1: S moves one step at a time, F two steps. They meet in the cycle.
    let (mut slow, mut fast) = (f(x0), f(f(x0)));
    while slow != fast {
        slow = f(slow);
        fast = f(f(fast));
    }
    // Phase 2: F restarts from x0 at speed 1. They meet at the beginning of the cycle.
    fast = x0;
    while slow != fast {
        slow = f(slow);
        fast = f(fast);
    }
    slow
}

/// Read-only duplicate: the cycle reached from position n starts at the duplicate.
/// Requires n >= 1 and every value in 0..n.
pub fn duplicate_floyd(a: &[usize]) -> usize {
    debug_assert!(a.len() >= 2 && a.iter().all(|&x| x < a.len() - 1));
    floyd(|i| a[i], a.len() - 1)
}

This finds a duplicate in \(\Theta(n)\) time and \(O(1)\) extra space, without modifying \(A.\)

Majority Element

Majority Element

We are given an array \(A[1, n].\) Find, if any, the element that occurs more than \(n/2\) times, that is, at least \(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 1\) times.

Such an element is called the majority element; there is at most one. Our running example is

\[A = [5, 3, 1, 1, 2, 3, 1, 1, 1],\]

where \(1\) occurs \(5\) times out of \(9,\) so it is the majority element.

Two easy solutions:

  • Sort (a copy of) \(A;\) the majority element, if any, occupies the middle position. \(\Theta(n \log n)\) time and \(\Theta(n)\) space.
  • Count the occurrences of each element with a HashMap. \(\Theta(n)\) expected time and \(\Theta(n)\) space.

Is \(\Theta(n)\) time and \(O(1)\) space possible?

Boyer-Moore algorithm
  • Keep a candidate \(c\) and a counter. At the beginning \(c = A[1]\) and the counter is \(1.\)
  • Process the array from left to right. For each element \(A[i]:\)
    • if \(A[i] = c,\) increase the counter by \(1;\)
    • otherwise, decrease the counter by \(1,\) and if the counter becomes \(0,\) set \(c = A[i]\) and the counter to \(1.\)

The figure shows the candidate and the counter after each element of our example.

At the end \(c = 1,\) the majority element. Notice that the counter, \(3,\) is not the frequency of \(1,\) which is \(5.\)

Claim. If there is a majority element \(e,\) then at the end \(c = e.\)

Why? Intuitively, we can use an occurrence of the majority element to “kill” another element: since \(e\) occurs more than all the other elements together, some occurrences of \(e\) survive. The algorithm does exactly this kind of killing: an element different from \(c\) kills one earlier occurrence of \(c\) that is still alive, and the two form a pair of different values. The elements still alive are the leftover; let \(L\) be their number. The following invariant holds after every step:

the elements read so far can be split into pairs of different values plus \(L\) leftover elements, all equal to \(c,\) and the counter is \(L\) or \(L + 1.\)

The counter is not always \(L\) because of our variant of the algorithm: when the counter would become \(0,\) the current element is taken as the new candidate with counter \(1,\) even if it has just been used in a pair. The figure shows the pairs formed on our example, and the counter and \(L\) after every element; keep it at hand while reading the cases below.

The invariant holds after the first element: no pairs, \(L = 1,\) leftover \(A[1] = c,\) counter \(1.\) Now suppose it holds before \(A[i].\)

  • \(A[i] = c:\) the new element joins the leftover. \(L\) and the counter both grow by \(1.\) This happens at elements 4, 8 and 9 of our example.
  • \(A[i] \neq c\) and counter \(\geq 2:\) then \(L \geq 1,\) because the counter is at most \(L + 1.\) \(A[i]\) forms a pair with a leftover element. \(L\) and the counter both decrease by \(1;\) the counter stays at least \(1,\) so \(c\) does not change. This happens at element 5.
  • \(A[i] \neq c,\) counter \(= 1\) and \(L = 1:\) \(A[i]\) forms a pair with the only leftover element, so now \(L = 0.\) The counter becomes \(0,\) so \(c = A[i]\) and the counter is \(1 = L + 1.\) This happens at elements 2 and 6 of our example.
  • \(A[i] \neq c,\) counter \(= 1\) and \(L = 0:\) there is nothing to pair with, so \(A[i]\) becomes the only leftover element, \(L = 1.\) The counter becomes \(0,\) so \(c = A[i]\) and the counter is \(1 = L.\) This happens at elements 3 and 7.

In every case the leftover elements are all equal to the new \(c,\) and the counter is \(L\) or \(L + 1.\)

Now, a pair contains two different values, so at most one occurrence of \(e.\) If all the occurrences of \(e\) were in pairs, each of them would be paired with a different element that is not \(e,\) and \(e\) would occur at most \(n/2\) times. So some occurrence of \(e\) is a leftover element, and all leftover elements are equal to \(c:\) thus \(c = e.\)

If there is no majority element, \(c\) can be anything, even the rarest element: on \([1, 1, 2, 2, 3]\) the candidate ends as \(3\) (the values of \(c\) are \(1, 1, 1, 2, 3\) and those of the counter \(1, 2, 1, 1, 1\)). Therefore the problem says “if any”: a second pass counts the occurrences of \(c\) and checks that they are more than \(n/2.\) In total, \(\Theta(n)\) time and \(O(1)\) space.

/// Boyer-Moore: if `a` has a majority element, it is the returned candidate.
pub fn majority_candidate(a: &[u32]) -> Option<u32> {
    let (&first, rest) = a.split_first()?;
    let (mut c, mut counter) = (first, 1);
    for &x in rest {
        if x == c {
            counter += 1;
        } else {
            counter -= 1;
            if counter == 0 {
                c = x;
                counter = 1;
            }
        }
    }
    Some(c)
}

/// The element occurring more than n/2 times, if any: a second pass checks the candidate.
pub fn majority(a: &[u32]) -> Option<u32> {
    let c = majority_candidate(a)?;
    let freq = a.iter().filter(|&&x| x == c).count();
    (freq > a.len() / 2).then_some(c)
}
Supporting insertions and deletions

Dynamic Majority Element

We have a multiset \(M\) of numbers, initially empty, and we would like to support the following operations:

  • insert(x) inserts the number \(x\) into \(M;\)
  • delete(x) deletes one occurrence of \(x\) from \(M;\)
  • majority() returns the element of \(M\) that occurs more than \(\lvert M \rvert / 2\) times, if any.

Boyer-Moore cannot delete. Its state is a single candidate and a counter, and when an element leaves we do not know which pairs it belonged to.

A different idea works: look at the bits. If \(e\) is the majority element, then in every bit position the bit of \(e\) is the majority bit, because the occurrences of \(e\) alone are more than half of the elements. So we keep, for every bit position, how many elements have a \(0\) and how many have a \(1\) in that position. The majority element, if there is one, is the number whose bits are the majority bits.

The left part of the figure shows our example, with \(3\) bits per element, the most significant bit first. The counts of zeros and ones give the bits \(0, 0, 1,\) that is, \(1.\) On the right we delete \(1\) five times and insert \(3:\) every operation changes one count per bit position. For each bit, the pair (zeros, ones) goes from \(A,\) to after the five deletions, to after the insertion, as follows: bit 2: \((8, 1) \to (3, 1) \to (4, 1);\) bit 1: \((6, 3) \to (1, 3) \to (1, 4);\) bit 0: \((1, 8) \to (1, 3) \to (1, 4).\) The new multiset is \([5, 3, 2, 3, 3],\) and the majority bits \(0, 1, 1\) give \(3,\) which indeed occurs \(3\) times out of \(5.\)

If \(u\) is the largest element, the numbers have \(\Theta(\log u)\) bits. Each insertion, deletion or query takes \(\Theta(\log u)\) time, and the space is \(\Theta(\log u)\) counters. In the code we keep only the number of ones and the size of the multiset: the number of zeros is their difference.

/// A multiset of numbers in [0, 2^bits) with insertions and deletions.
/// For every bit position b we count the elements whose bit b is 1;
/// the zeros are len - ones[b]. Θ(log u) time per operation, Θ(log u) space.
pub struct BitMajority {
    ones: Vec<usize>,
    len: usize,
}

impl BitMajority {
    /// Numbers must be smaller than 2^bits, with bits <= 32.
    pub fn new(bits: usize) -> Self {
        assert!(bits <= 32);
        BitMajority { ones: vec![0; bits], len: 0 }
    }

    pub fn insert(&mut self, x: u32) {
        debug_assert!(self.ones.len() == 32 || x >> self.ones.len() == 0);
        self.len += 1;
        for (b, cnt) in self.ones.iter_mut().enumerate() {
            *cnt += ((x >> b) & 1) as usize;
        }
    }

    /// `x` must be in the multiset.
    pub fn delete(&mut self, x: u32) {
        self.len -= 1;
        for (b, cnt) in self.ones.iter_mut().enumerate() {
            *cnt -= ((x >> b) & 1) as usize;
        }
    }

    /// Bit b of the answer is the majority bit in position b.
    /// It is the majority element if the multiset has one.
    pub fn candidate(&self) -> u32 {
        let mut c = 0;
        for (b, &cnt) in self.ones.iter().enumerate() {
            if cnt > self.len - cnt {
                c |= 1 << b;
            }
        }
        c
    }
}

As for Boyer-Moore, the answer is correct only if a majority element exists. Otherwise the majority bits may give any number, even one that is not in the multiset.

Misra-Gries Heavy Hitters

Heavy Hitters

Given an array \(A[1, n]\) and an integer \(T,\) find the elements that occur more than \(n/T\) times, that is, at least \(\lfloor n/T \rfloor + 1\) times.

The majority element is the case \(T = 2.\) There are fewer than \(T\) such elements, because \(T\) of them would occupy more than \(n\) positions. These elements are called heavy hitters.

Misra and Gries generalize the candidate of Boyer-Moore: we keep a set \(K\) of at most \(T\) candidates, each with a counter, in a hash map from element to counter. For each element \(e\) of \(A:\)

  • if \(e\) is in \(K,\) we increase its counter by \(1;\)
  • otherwise we insert \(e\) in \(K\) with counter \(1,\) and if now \(K\) has more than \(T\) elements, we decrease every counter in \(K\) by \(1\) and remove the elements whose counter becomes \(0.\) We call this a round. The new element, with counter \(1,\) is always removed.

The figure follows the algorithm on the array of the previous section with \(T = 2\) counters (one more than the majority needs, as we will see); below every element, the content of \(K\) after processing it.

There are \(2\) rounds, and at the end \(K\) contains only \(1,\) with counter \(3.\)

Why is it correct? A round decreases \(T+1\) counters, of \(T+1\) distinct elements, by \(1.\) Every element of \(A\) increases one counter by \(1,\) so the counters receive \(n\) units in total, and the rounds cannot remove more than \(n\) units. Hence there are at most \(n/(T+1)\) rounds. A round decreases the counter of an element \(x\) at most once, so if \(x\) occurs \(f_x\) times, its final counter is at least \(f_x - n/(T+1).\) Thus, every element with \(f_x > n/(T+1)\) is still in \(K\) at the end. In particular, every element occurring more than \(n/T\) times is in \(K.\)

In our example \(n/(T+1) = 3,\) and \(1,\) which occurs \(5\) times, ends with counter \(5 - 2 = 3.\)

As for Boyer-Moore, the converse does not hold: an element of \(K\) may be rare, and the counters underestimate the frequencies. So a second pass counts the occurrences of the at most \(T\) candidates and keeps those occurring more than \(n/T\) times. Notice that the letter \(T\) plays two roles: the threshold \(n/T\) of the problem and the number of counters. They need not be equal. Since \(T\) counters find every element occurring more than \(n/(T+1)\) times, the problem with threshold \(n/T\) needs only \(T - 1\) counters; we keep \(T\) as in the lecture. For the majority element (threshold \(n/2\)) one counter suffices: Misra-Gries with one counter is a version of Boyer-Moore, which differs from ours only in this: when the counter would become \(0,\) the candidate is dropped and the next element becomes the new candidate.

/// Misra-Gries with T counters. Every element occurring more than n/(T+1)
/// times is a key of the result. Θ(n) expected time, Θ(T) space.
pub fn misra_gries(a: &[u32], t: usize) -> HashMap<u32, usize> {
    let mut k: HashMap<u32, usize> = HashMap::new();
    for &e in a {
        *k.entry(e).or_insert(0) += 1;
        if k.len() > t {
            // T + 1 distinct candidates: decrement all, drop the zeros.
            k.retain(|_, c| {
                *c -= 1;
                *c > 0
            });
        }
    }
    k
}

/// The elements occurring more than n/T times (at least ⌊n/T⌋ + 1 times),
/// sorted. One more pass counts exactly the occurrences of the candidates.
pub fn heavy_hitters(a: &[u32], t: usize) -> Vec<u32> {
    let mut k = misra_gries(a, t);
    k.values_mut().for_each(|c| *c = 0);
    for x in a {
        if let Some(c) = k.get_mut(x) {
            *c += 1;
        }
    }
    let mut out: Vec<u32> = k.into_iter().filter(|&(_, f)| f * t > a.len()).map(|(x, _)| x).collect();
    out.sort_unstable();
    out
}

Running time. A naive analysis gives \(O(T \cdot n)\) time: we process \(n\) elements, and a round scans the \(T+1\) counters. But the time is actually \(\Theta(n)\) (expected, because of the hash map). A round costs \(\Theta(T+1)\) and removes \(T+1\) units from the counters; since only \(n\) units are ever added, the total cost of all the rounds is \(O(n):\) it cannot exceed the number of elements of the array. The space is \(\Theta(T).\)

A Last Pearl: Dominoes on a Chessboard

Dominoes on a Chessboard

We remove two opposite corners from a chessboard. Is it possible to cover the remaining \(62\) squares with \(31\) dominoes, each covering two adjacent squares?

Think about it before reading on. Trying to place the dominoes leads nowhere: there are too many ways.

The answer is no, and the argument is about colours, not about dominoes. Two opposite corners have the same colour, light in the figure. So the mutilated board has \(32\) dark squares and only \(30\) light squares. Every domino, horizontal or vertical, covers one dark and one light square. Thus \(31\) dominoes would cover \(31\) dark and \(31\) light squares, but there are only \(30\) light ones.

Removing Two Squares of Different Colours

Removing Two Squares of Different Colours

Now we remove two squares of different colours, anywhere on the board. Can we always cover the remaining \(62\) squares with \(31\) dominoes?

Counting colours is of no help now: \(31\) dark and \(31\) light squares remain. The answer is yes, for every choice of the two squares, and the proof is again a picture.

Thread a necklace through the board: a closed path that visits each of the \(64\) squares exactly once, always moving to an adjacent square. The left figure above shows one: the top row from left to right, then a snake down through the other columns, and back up along the first column. Adjacent squares have different colours, so the colours alternate along the necklace.

Now remove two squares of different colours. The necklace is cut in two points and falls into two pieces. Number the squares along the necklace from \(0\) to \(63.\) Since the colours alternate, the two removed squares are at positions \(p < q\) with \(q - p\) odd. One piece holds the \(q - p - 1\) squares between them, the other the remaining \(64 - (q - p) - 1\) squares, and both numbers are even. (A piece is empty when the two squares are consecutive on the necklace.) Each piece is a path of adjacent squares of even length, so we cut it into dominoes, taking its squares two at a time from one end.

In the right figure above we remove the squares \((1, 5)\) and \((5, 2)\) (row and column, counting from \(0\) at the top left). The two pieces have \(30\) and \(32\) squares, and become \(15\) and \(16\) dominoes.
